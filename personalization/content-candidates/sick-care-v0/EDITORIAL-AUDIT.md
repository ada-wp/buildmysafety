# Review of the existing thirty sick-day tasks

October 14, 2026. Ada requested analysis of the actual saved thirty suggestions after the discussion of situation-led discovery and copyable research questions. This is an editorial review of [pool.json](pool.json), not an invented bad/good demonstration, a rewrite of the collection, a new market study, or a live model test.

All thirty full records were read: titles, summaries, steps, completion conditions, fit guidance and resource references. The [runtime mapper](../../sick-care-catalog.mjs) was also inspected to distinguish saved copy from its overrides. Thirty saved candidates include three parked work/business tasks, SC22–SC24. The mapper imports the other twenty-seven and applies some conditional copy. The broader app also retains an earlier sitter-search task; that is outside this thirty-task audit.

## Overall judgment

The pool is not uniformly weak. Some tasks already uncover useful distinctions: care estimates, refill versus renewal, employer benefits, pet-care formats and veterinary permissions. Those should not be discarded just because the new writing examples feel better.

The weaker cards often send the user to a website and ask her to inspect it, without first contributing enough knowledge or opening up useful alternative routes. Several promise preparation but mainly deliver research. Others turn a narrow convenience into a whole standalone task. These are editorial judgments against Ada's stated standards, not measured customer ratings or claims about market prevalence.

Recommended disposition: **7 substantive rewrites; 2 candidates for integration into a broader task or contextual detail; 3 useful later-stage cards to simplify or target carefully; 15 concepts worth keeping with appropriate copy/format refinements; 3 already parked outside the sick-day default flow.** None of these recommendations changes eligibility, ranking, saved tasks or live content.

## Seven cards to rewrite first

### SC07 — Find out whether a pharmacy can reach your door

**Actual wording:** “Choose a local pharmacy from your plan's directory, if insured, or an official pharmacy site.”

The summary acknowledges new prescriptions versus mail-order refills, but the body explores one local pharmacy. The distinction does not turn into genuinely different routes. It misses the opportunity to explain online pharmacies, health-plan mail order and local courier delivery, then help her investigate whichever fits her timing and circumstances. Its only linked example is CVS.

**Why it falls short:** This is the exact narrow-search pattern Ada challenged. It effectively asks the user to discover the wider landscape herself.

**Revision direction:** Introduce distinct acquisition routes and their purposes before three research questions. Only use routine-refill guidance when relevant. Keep provider eligibility and timely availability unconfirmed until checked. Do not fix this simply by adding a second pharmacy brand.

### SC21 — Discover local help beyond people you already know

**Actual wording:** “Open your local 211 website and see which support areas it covers, such as food access or transportation.”

The source is useful and its referral limitation is stated honestly. But the activity is mostly a directory tour: open the site, read a listing, find how to contact 211. The user is still responsible for knowing what kind of help to look for and judging whether it is worth investigating.

**Why it falls short:** The card title promises discovery; the body delivers navigation instructions. A useful starting source alone is not a complete solution.

**Revision direction:** Start with her specific obstacle, introduce relevant kinds of support, then offer questions about the route, eligibility and obtaining the help. Keep local availability uncertain. Do not promise that 211 itself supplies services or can resolve any arbitrary errand.

### SC15 — Find a reliable answer to food-storage questions

**Actual wording:** “Identify where you'd check again after opening or preparing that food.”

FoodKeeper is a concrete reference and the task correctly notes that guidance depends on the food and its condition. However, the three steps largely teach one lookup and how to repeat it. The card does not strongly connect that lookup to having usable food when the person needs rest.

**Why it falls short:** The completion is mainly familiarity with a tool. For a first sick-day experience, the useful decision needs to be clearer.

**Revision direction:** Anchor the learning in a real preparation decision, such as choosing food that will still be usable when needed and understanding unopened, opened or prepared storage guidance. Supply verified knowledge and three questions that help with that decision. Retain the narrow lookup version only for someone who actually asks a storage question. Do not invent storage durations or decide whether a particular food is safe.

### SC14 — Take one last-minute shop off your future plate

**Actual wording:** “Pick a nonmedical essential you regularly use and have run out of, such as tissues, menstrual products or pet litter.”

The remaining steps are to buy a modest spare and replenish it when opened. This can solve an explicitly recurring shortage. Its existing Extra ease priority is appropriate; the issue is not that the app currently calls it universally essential.

**Why it falls short:** As a general discovery card, it adds little beyond “keep a spare.” It depends on the user supplying the insight, and can make the collection feel padded if used as an early recommendation without that stated gap.

**Revision direction:** Keep it tightly tied to a known shortage, or incorporate the useful material preparation into a broader task. For general preparation, reveal a real overlooked dependency rather than require arbitrary stockpiling. Existing possessions and budget should shape it; buying something is not the measure of progress.

### SC01 — Find a care option you can actually start with

**Actual wording:** “With insurance, look for virtual care in your plan or benefits portal. Without a suitable benefit, try HRSA's health-center finder.”

This is an important, generally sensible task, not an idea to remove. The first-visit and cost questions are useful. However, the main body is a fairly rigid two-route choice followed by contact and recordkeeping. Self-pay virtual care appears only in the internal variants field. The completion condition requires confirmed access and cost, sometimes depending on a reply.

**Why it falls short:** The app could first teach the different entry routes and help her select one. The current card asks for practical verification before doing enough to explain the choices. It also blurs introductory research and establishing an access route.

**Revision direction:** For discovery, give three copyable questions about relevant benefits, self-pay online care and community care, with a useful explanation and a learning outcome. If she explicitly wants to establish a route, use a preparation variant and keep its honest confirmation requirement. Do not require all three routes if one is inapplicable.

**Runtime distinction:** The app mapper already softens the opening so it does not declare that she lacks a regular doctor. The main steps and completion condition are still inherited from the pool.

### SC12 — Choose meal delivery that actually removes cooking

**Actual wording:** “Compare a meal kit such as HelloFresh with prepared meals such as Factor: ingredients to cook versus meals to heat.”

The meal-kit/prepared-meal distinction is a useful insight. The card also includes recurring charges, equipment and cancellation. It does mention grocery-store prepared meals, but that route is secondary to the two subscription-brand examples.

**Why it falls short:** The actual need is food with little effort. Leading with two commercial formats can narrow exploration too early, particularly for occasional sick days, small orders or limited budgets.

**Revision direction:** Compare different ways to meet the need: a non-subscription prepared-food option, relevant prepared-meal services, or food that can be available at home before illness. Give copyable questions about preparation effort, minimum commitment and total cost. Keep brand names secondary to the problem. Do not claim particular routes are always cheaper or accessible.

### SC18 — Find help for an errand you can't postpone

**Actual saved wording:** “Choose one nonmedical errand, such as collecting a repaired item. For paid help, try Taskrabbit; for community options, contact local 211.”

The task appropriately starts with a real job and checks collection permissions. But its main example is weakly connected to ordinary sick-day preparation, and “paid help or 211” is too coarse to explain different routes. The saved completion also requires a provider confirmation while the steps combine investigation and contact collection.

**Why it falls short:** It does not sufficiently explain whether the person needs a retailer's service, a specialist service, an errand runner or eligible community support. It puts most solution discovery back on her.

**Revision direction:** Start with the specific responsibility she cannot manage. Introduce plausible service types that solve that responsibility and the practical details that make them usable. Choose research or actual arrangement as the intended outcome, with matching completion wording.

**Runtime distinction:** The mapper already replaces the repaired-item example with a broader ordinary errand, and changes completion to understanding a promising route and outstanding questions. Do not report the saved provider-confirmation wording as the current displayed copy. The broader need for concrete alternative routes remains.

## Useful pieces that may fit better inside another task

- **SC09 — Learn how prescription collection by someone else works.** This is a real option with a useful permissions distinction. Its suitability depends on a trusted willing person eventually being available. Consider it a contextual alternative in the prescription-access task, or a separate card only when this is the user's actual question. Do not routinely make someone with no available helper read about an unavailable route. This is a prioritization suggestion, not an automatic exclusion of discovery.
- **SC20 — Learn which kind of home help fits.** It offers a worthwhile distinction between household help, personal assistance and healthcare, but feels abstract when presented before a concrete problem. Consider incorporating the distinction in a more specific help-discovery task. Preserve professional assessment boundaries when personal or clinical care is relevant; ordinary cleaners must not be presented as substitutes.

## Collection-level problems

1. **Thirty records do not guarantee thirty distinct useful starting points.** Three are already parked, several require a chosen provider/carer or a planned procedure, and some cover neighboring parts of the same larger need. Keep specific follow-ups, but do not treat them as interchangeable first recommendations.
2. **Material preparation is thin.** The main direct physical-preparation cards are the resting setup (SC13) and spare essential (SC14). Much of the collection is investigating, confirming or saving information. There is no dedicated card in these thirty that leaves two genuinely usable low-effort meals ready, even though material preparation is part of the philosophy. Adding a task still needs a real distinct outcome, not padding.
3. **Research should contribute knowledge before sending her away.** Several existing cards already do this well. The weaker ones need stronger substance, not just punctuation changes from instructions into questions.
4. **Completion needs to match the promise.** Learning can end with understanding a route or knowing it is unsuitable. Arranging care/help must still require real agreement. Do not remove honest confirmation merely to make every card easier to check off.
5. **The prompt and pool require separate work.** Ordinary suggestions use saved pool copy. A revised AI prompt will not improve those stored cards by itself. These thirty are authored candidates, not evidence that the API generated them.

## Proposed next editing pass

First rewrite SC07, SC21 and SC15 together for review: they demonstrate narrow solution search, delegated discovery and a weakly connected learning outcome. Then revisit SC01, SC12, SC14 and SC18. Preserve the strong concepts and contextual follow-ups. Do not rewrite all thirty indiscriminately or remove useful basics solely because they are familiar.
