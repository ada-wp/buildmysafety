## What the original comparison missed

The previous comparison overweighted emergency-response products. Finch and Fabulous demonstrate that **encouragement, structure and a process for small actions are established offers**, not just incidental features of emergency tools. Their published paid tiers show businesses offer subscriptions for these experiences. This review did not verify revenue, paid conversion, retention or women-living-alone usage, so offer existence must not be represented as confirmed willingness to pay for our combined product.

The coherent distinction to test is: **help women build an independent life they trust, by turning one uncertainty into a practiced capability.** General self-care apps can help someone feel encouraged; a generic task tool can split an instruction. Our proposed product would connect knowledge, people, resources and systems to a real-world outcome, then help the user try it and revise it.

An example Know / Act / Trust loop:
- **Know:** understand what a sick day would interrupt and what help is actually available.
- **Act:** prepare food/access information and arrange a specific support option.
- **Trust:** rehearse the arrangement, notice what worked, and keep a small record of “I now know how to handle this.”

That distinction is a hypothesis, not a defensible moat yet. A warm interface, personalized text, or a seven-day schedule is easy to copy. The assets worth building are the reviewed action/resource library, constraint-aware routing, usable templates, respectful guidance and evidence that users complete and retain useful capabilities.
